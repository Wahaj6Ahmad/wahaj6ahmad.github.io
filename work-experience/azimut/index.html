What my job actually looked like

At Azimut Wavetec, I worked on electromechanical kiosks and machines that ship to real customers. The work was very end-to-end. My involvement started the moment a customer order landed and usually ended when the system was installed and stable in the field.

If I had to summarize the role in one line: I was the person who made sure the hardware solution was not only designed, but also buildable, testable, documented, shipped, installable, and supportable. If it had a connector, a cable, or a protocol… it eventually became my problem (and I mean that in a good way).

Field support: troubleshooting breakdowns on-site with customer technical teams.
Module validation: testing liveness camera IR behavior before it becomes a production headache.

What I owned

  • Requirements handoff: confirmed hardware requirement documents with business analysts and project managers, and clarified edge cases early.
  • BOM ownership: created BOMs, coordinated procurement details, and aligned part availability with build timelines.
  • Factory + CAD coordination: sent specs to factory/CAD, reviewed 3D designs in detail, and approved builds only when serviceability + manufacturability made sense.
  • Custom cabling: designed custom cable sets (lengths, materials, connectors), ordered through vendors, and verified wiring correctness in real assemblies.
  • Module integration: sourced and integrated new hardware modules (POS terminals, scanners, EMV readers, biometric devices) for customer-specific features.
  • Software bridging: worked with software teams by providing module documentation, communication protocols, and integration guidance (so hardware actually worked in production code).
  • System images: prepared final OS images for factory installation across kiosks.
  • QC mindset: created QC checklists, performed checks myself or supervised the process, and pushed fixes upstream (factory + software) to prevent repeats.
  • Deployment + support: provided installation guides, supported overseas deployments, and handled breakdowns in the field.

Highlights and impact

  • International readiness: integrated and validated modules (POS terminals, scanners, EMV readers, smart card/biometric devices) for international customer projects.
  • Field-first debugging: troubleshot integration and deployment issues hands-on, performed root-cause analysis, and drove fixes with factory and software teams to prevent repeats.
  • Repeatability: standardized installation and troubleshooting docs into clearer work instructions so deployments didn’t depend on “that one person who remembers everything.”
  • People impact: mentored two junior engineers across hardware and technical support, helping them plan work and resolve issues faster.
QC in practice: validating camera behavior during build checks.
Everyday reality: module repair and diagnostics with circuitry exposed. This is where you learn the system for real.
Toolbox (what I used day-to-day)
  • Hardware lifecycle: requirements review, BOM creation, vendor coordination, CAD review, custom cable design, assembly support
  • Systems integration: module comms + software handoff (protocol docs, integration notes, troubleshooting steps)
  • Production readiness: OS image preparation, QC checklists, installation guides, maintenance manuals
  • Field support: on-site troubleshooting with customer technical teams, root-cause analysis, fix validation

What I learned (and still carry)

The most important lesson: “end-to-end ownership” is a superpower. When you understand the system from order to deployment, you make better design calls, you document better, and you debug faster because you know where the hidden assumptions live.

An important thing I learned on the job. Now you can appreciate too.
