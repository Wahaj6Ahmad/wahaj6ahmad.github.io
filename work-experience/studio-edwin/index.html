Embedded Systems Engineering Intern (Sep 2023 - Jan 2024): Autonomous audio recorder for Spiral of Time (installed at MACBA)

The spiral-shaped interface of Spiral of Time - photo © Edwin van der Heide

From Sep 2023 to Jan 2024, I interned at Studio Edwin van der Heide in Rotterdam. The internship centered around Spiral of Time, a sound-based artwork that builds a long-term archive of a place by recording one minute of ambient audio every hour, day and night, over multiple years.

The work is now installed at the Museu d'Art Contemporani de Barcelona (MACBA), where a dedicated microphone setup registers the ambient sound of Plaça dels Àngels and feeds the growing sound library that visitors can explore. You can read the installation description here: MACBA — Spiral of Time (Plaça dels Àngels) .

My job was to help build the Autonomous Audio Recorder (AAR) — the part of the system that makes this project possible. In simple terms: it had to wake up on schedule, record clean audio, upload files, and keep running for long periods with minimal human help.

Some implementation details are classified (specific modules and internal configuration), but the engineering constraints, trade-offs, and reliability work were very real.

What I built (high-level)

The AAR is a solar-powered, Linux-based embedded system designed for reliable long-term autonomy. It records audio at strict hourly intervals, generates metadata for the web experience, and uploads everything to a remote server. Remote access was a must, because the system is meant to run at a distance.

The Problems

  • Timing reliability: the recorder had to wake up at the correct time every hour, without drifting, and recover after power loss.
  • Power and autonomy: it had to survive real weather and battery constraints, and avoid corrupting data during brownouts or sudden shutdowns.
  • Audio quality: recordings had to be clean and consistent — no random glitches, even under system load.
  • Remote maintenance: we needed reliable remote access for debugging and updates while keeping the system secure.

My approach

The project was built through iterative prototyping and daily robustness testing. We tried approaches, hit edge cases, and refined the system until it behaved consistently. One example: early wake-up solutions were unreliable in practice, so we changed strategy and moved to a simpler, more robust approach based on RTC alarm scheduling and careful shutdown sequencing.

On the software side, I worked with a mix of Linux services, shell scripts, Python scripts, and audio tooling. The system maintains logs for traceability and uses efficient file transfer so uploads stay reliable even with unstable connections.

Final Product

The final system was able to run autonomously and record consistently at the required hourly schedule during extended testing, with high-quality audio and reliable upload behavior. We also packaged the software setup into a repeatable installation image and documentation so the system could be reproduced and scaled.

My biggest takeaway

This internship changed how I think about engineering. I came in expecting “interactive art design” and ended up deep in reliability, autonomy, and deployment realities — and I loved it. It also showed me something I didn’t appreciate enough before: technology can be built for purely creative goals, and still demand serious engineering.

A personal milestone: seeing this work installed at MACBA felt unreal. It’s one thing to prototype; it’s another to ship something that must keep working in the real world for years. The next destination for Spiral of Time is the Amazon forest in Brazil! Feels surreal!

Why this matters for my robotics path

  • Systems integration: hardware + Linux services + scheduling + networking as one reliable product loop.
  • Reliability mindset: treating edge cases as first-class requirements (power loss, drift, timing, silent failures).
  • Remote deployment thinking: logs, maintainability, secure access, and reproducibility.
