Embedded Systems Engineering Intern (Sep 2023 - Jan 2024). Autonomous audio recorder for Spiral of Time (installed at MACBA)

The Spiral of Time.

From Sep 2023 to Jan 2024, I interned at Studio Edwin van der Heide in Rotterdam. The internship centered around Spiral of Time, a sound-based artwork that builds a long-term archive of a place by recording one minute of ambient audio every hour, day and night, over multiple years. :contentReference[oaicite:0]{index=0}

The work is now installed at the Museu d'Art Contemporani de Barcelona (MACBA), where a dedicated microphone setup registers the ambient sound of Plaça dels Àngels and feeds the growing sound library that visitors can explore. You can read the installation description here: MACBA — Spiral of Time (Plaça dels Àngels) .

My job was to help build the Autonomous Audio Recorder (AAR) — the part of the system that makes this project possible. In simple terms: it had to wake up on schedule, record clean audio, upload files, and keep running for long periods with minimal human help. :contentReference[oaicite:2]{index=2}

Some implementation details are classified (specific modules and internal configuration), but the engineering constraints, trade-offs, and reliability work were very real.

What I built (high-level)

The AAR is a solar-powered, Linux-based embedded system designed for reliable long-term autonomy. It records audio at strict hourly intervals, generates metadata for the web experience, and uploads everything to a remote server. Remote access was a must, because the system is meant to run at a distance. :contentReference[oaicite:3]{index=3}

The problems that mattered

  • Timing reliability: the recorder had to wake up at the correct time every hour, without drifting, and recover after power loss. :contentReference[oaicite:4]{index=4}
  • Power and autonomy: it had to survive real weather and battery constraints, and avoid corrupting data during brownouts or sudden shutdowns. :contentReference[oaicite:5]{index=5}
  • Audio quality: recordings had to be clean and consistent — no random glitches, even under system load. :contentReference[oaicite:6]{index=6}
  • Remote maintenance: we needed reliable remote access for debugging and updates while keeping the system secure. :contentReference[oaicite:7]{index=7}

How I approached it

The project was built through iterative prototyping and daily robustness testing. We tried approaches, hit edge cases, and refined the system until it behaved consistently. :contentReference[oaicite:8]{index=8} One example: early wake-up solutions were unreliable in practice, so we changed strategy and moved to a simpler, more robust approach based on RTC alarm scheduling and careful shutdown sequencing. :contentReference[oaicite:9]{index=9}

On the software side, I worked with a mix of Linux services, shell scripts, Python scripts, and audio tooling. The system maintains logs for traceability and uses efficient file transfer so uploads stay reliable even with unstable connections. :contentReference[oaicite:10]{index=10}

What shipped

The final system was able to run autonomously and record consistently at the required hourly schedule during extended testing, with high-quality audio and reliable upload behavior. :contentReference[oaicite:11]{index=11} We also packaged the software setup into a repeatable installation image and documentation so the system could be reproduced and scaled. :contentReference[oaicite:12]{index=12}

My biggest takeaway

This internship changed how I think about engineering. I came in expecting “interactive art design” and ended up deep in reliability, autonomy, and deployment realities — and I loved it. It also showed me something I didn’t appreciate enough before: technology can be built for purely creative goals, and still demand serious engineering.

A personal milestone: seeing this work installed at MACBA felt unreal. It’s one thing to prototype; it’s another to ship something that must keep working in the real world for years.

Why this matters for my robotics path

  • Systems integration: hardware + Linux services + scheduling + networking as one reliable product loop.
  • Reliability mindset: treating edge cases as first-class requirements (power loss, drift, timing, silent failures).
  • Remote deployment thinking: logs, maintainability, secure access, and reproducibility.

If you want the short version of my LinkedIn post: I’m proud this went live, and I’m still a bit surprised how much joy I got from making a box wake up on time.
